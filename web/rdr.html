Working with RDRs

An RDR records a decision: the problem, what was learned, the options, the choice. This page shows how RDRs are used day to day with Claude Code and Nexus. Not the formal process. The natural one: a conversation that gets written down, grows over several sessions, is checked, is built, and is closed.

Words in <ORANGE CAPITALS IN ANGLE BRACKETS> are placeholders for your own subject. The prompts are examples of phrasing, not scripts. Read Getting started first, in particular lesson 2 on continuations and lesson 8 on renderings. Both are used on every page here.

1

What an RDR is here

5 min · reading only

RDR means Research, Design, Review. The name describes the order of the work. You research first, then you design, then the design is reviewed. The document is a rendering of that work, in the sense of Getting started lesson 8: one readable file, written from what is in the stores, that changes as the work changes.

Why write one

A decision of any size does not fit in one head, or in one Claude session. Without a written decision, the purpose drifts while the code is written, side problems take over, and three weeks later nobody knows why a choice was made. An RDR puts the thinking before the code, so the code has a fixed target. And because Nexus indexes every RDR, the next decision on a similar subject finds this one.

What it is not

It is not a form to fill. It is not a test plan. It is not a promise to a manager. A bug with one clear cause gets three sections: Problem, Root Cause, Fix. A design with real alternatives gets the full template. The length follows the decision.

In practice the danger runs the other way: an RDR grows. Sections accrete, each defensible on its own, until the document is longer than the decision. The discipline that RDRs need is cutting, not filling. When Claude adds a section, ask what decision it serves. If the answer is none, remove it.

The lifecycle

StatusMeaning
draftUnder discussion. Change it freely. Do not quote it as a decision yet.
acceptedThe gate passed and you accepted it. This is the design. Code can start.
deferredParked. It can come back, but only to draft.
closedBuilt and shipped. The RDR is now history, and indexed into knowledge.
supersededReplaced by a named later RDR.
abandonedNot going to happen.

Who does what. Claude does the work: research, drafting, fixing what a review finds, decomposing into tasks, building. You move the lifecycle: you say when to run the gate, when to accept, when to close, when to abandon. This boundary is not stable by itself. Claude crosses it, because running the next step looks like finishing the work. In the Nexus project it happened twice in one week of September 2026. The sentence that stops it: "Finish this stage and stop. I move the lifecycle." Say it early, and again when needed.

Investigate further

  • Ask Claude: "List the RDRs in this project and their statuses." In a new project the list is empty. In the Nexus repository it is over two hundred.
  • RDR: the full template and the reasoning behind it.
  • RDR-205: a real, large one, accepted in September 2026. Read the Problem Statement and the Approach only.
2

It starts as a conversation

10 min

An RDR does not begin with a template. It begins when you and Claude are talking about a problem and one of you notices that the problem is bigger than a quick fix. At that moment the conversation becomes the first draft.

The moment

You are describing a problem. Claude asks questions, proposes something, you push back. Two or three exchanges in, it is clear that there are several ways to do this, or that the cause is not known yet, or that other things depend on it. Then:

You might say
This is bigger than a patch. Let's make it an RDR. Start from what we just discussed.
What Claude does

Claude creates the file with /conexus:rdr-create: the next number, the frontmatter, the section headings, status draft. It fills the Problem Statement from the conversation so far, and lists the open questions the conversation raised. It also writes a memory entry (Getting started, lesson 4) that names the RDR, so later sessions know it exists. Nothing else is written yet. The rest of the sections are empty on purpose.

Claude may also propose the RDR itself: "this has three viable designs, do you want an RDR?" The plugin's brainstorming guidance tells it to ask before building anything with no design of record. Say yes or no.

Right-size it now

Tell Claude how big the decision is, because that sets how much of the template is used:

This is a bug with one cause. Problem, root cause, fix. Nothing else.
This is an architecture change. Use the full template, and we will need real alternatives.

End the session properly

An RDR of any size takes more than one session. At the end of this first one, run /conexus:continuation (Getting started, lesson 2). The handoff names the RDR file, the open questions, and the first research to do. The next session picks it up from there.

Investigate further

  • Ask Claude to show the new file: "Show me RDR <NUMBER> as it stands." It is short. That is correct at this stage.
  • The brainstorming gate: why Claude asks before building anything without a design of record.
3

Research, in cycles

20 min

Research is the R in RDR, and it is where the time goes. It is not one step. It is a cycle: ask, read what came back, ask the next thing. Each cycle adds findings to the RDR. Claude does the searching. You decide what to look at next.

A cycle

You might say
Research how <THE THING THE DECISION DEPENDS ON> actually behaves. Check our code, our knowledge store, and the outside sources. Add what you find to RDR <NUMBER>.
What Claude does

Claude starts the research agent (/conexus:research). The agent searches the knowledge store first, because an earlier RDR may have settled the question. Then it searches the code, then the web. When it returns, Claude adds each finding to the RDR with /conexus:rdr-research add, and each finding carries a tag:

TagMeaning
VerifiedConfirmed by reading the code, or by a small program that was actually run.
DocumentedSupported by documentation only. Not checked against reality.
AssumedBelieved, not checked. The design may depend on it.

The tags matter later. An Assumed finding that the design depends on is a Critical Assumption. The gate (lesson 5) looks at exactly those. This section is the easiest one to leave empty, and an empty one is how an unchecked belief reaches the design. When the design rests on something nobody checked, ask for it by name: "List the assumptions this design depends on, and mark which are verified."

The next cycle

Read what came back. Not the whole RDR, only the new findings. Something in them raises the next question. Ask it. Typical next questions:

  • "That finding is Assumed. Can you verify it? Write a small test or read the source."
  • "You found that <X>. Does that rule out option <B>?"
  • "We did something like this in RDR <OLDER NUMBER>. What did we learn there?"
  • "Measure it. How long does <THE OPERATION> take now?"

Three to six cycles is normal for a design of medium size. Some cycles are five minutes. Some are a whole session, when a finding needs a small program written and run to verify it.

Verify in batches

When several Assumed findings need a small program to verify them, do them together, in one branch and one test run, not one at a time. A test run costs the same for one check or five. Say so: "Collect the assumptions that need a spike. Verify them together."

Alternatives appear here, not later. As research shows what is possible, the options become clear. Have Claude write each one into Alternatives Considered as soon as it is clear, with the reason it is or is not attractive. An RDR whose alternatives are written after the decision is a justification, not a decision.

Investigate further

  • Ask Claude: "Which findings in RDR <NUMBER> are still Assumed, and which of those does the design depend on?"
  • The research skill: how findings are recorded and verified.
  • Evidence classification: the three tags and why they exist.
4

Read it, react, repeat

15 min

Between research cycles, and especially when the Proposed Solution starts to form, you read the RDR as a whole and react to it. This is the rendering loop from Getting started lesson 8, applied to one document. You do not edit the RDR line by line. You say what is wrong, and Claude changes the document and the stores together.

Ask for a reading

You might say
Give me RDR <NUMBER> as it stands. Problem, what we know, the options, and where the design is leaning.

Claude renders the current state. Read it as a reader, not as an author. Where does it lose you? Where does a claim have no finding behind it? Where does an option get one line while another gets a page?

React

Say what you see, in your own words. Examples of the kind of thing that moves an RDR:

  • "The problem statement is about performance, but the findings are all about correctness. Which is it?"
  • "Option B is dismissed in one sentence. Give it the same treatment as A."
  • "That number in the approach, where does it come from? I do not see a finding for it."
  • "This section repeats the last one. Cut it."
  • "I think the real constraint is <X>. Does the research support that?"

Claude changes the document. When the change is a fact, a count, a name, a decision, it also changes every other place that fact appears, in the RDR and in memory. A fact has one home. If you see the same fact stated two ways, that is a defect. Say so.

Bring in a second reader

You might say
Have the critic read RDR <NUMBER> before I do. Structural problems, unsupported claims, missing alternatives.

Claude starts the critic agent (/conexus:substantive-critique). It reads the RDR and reports what it finds, ranked. Claude fixes what is clearly wrong and shows you what needs a decision. This is cheaper than the gate (lesson 5) and you can do it as often as you like. Many RDRs go through two or three critic readings before anyone mentions a gate.

Drafts are for changing. While the status is draft, nothing is fixed. Change the problem statement. Drop an option. Split the RDR in two when research shows two decisions hiding in one. The chain of changes is not visible in the final document, and it does not need to be. The stores and the git history carry it.

Investigate further

  • Ask for a rendering aimed at a reader who was not in the conversation: "Render RDR <NUMBER> for a new team member." Gaps that you no longer see become visible.
  • The critic agent: what it looks for.
5

The gate

15 min · read even if you never run one

The gate is a formal check before acceptance: does the document hold together, are the critical assumptions verified, and does an independent critique find anything that blocks. Not every RDR needs it. A small decision that the reading loop in lesson 4 has already tested can go from draft to accepted on that reading alone. Use the gate for the decisions that are expensive to reverse: a schema, a wire contract, a migration. When you use it, treat the rounds as a budget, not as a loop to run until it is clean; a critique will always find something, and past the second round what it finds is cheaper to discover by building.

You say when

You might say
Run the gate on RDR <NUMBER>. Budget: two rounds.
What Claude does

Claude runs /conexus:rdr-gate. Three checks: the structure (are the required sections present and consistent), the assumptions (is every Critical Assumption verified or explicitly accepted), and a critique by an independent agent. The result is PASSED, or BLOCKED with findings ranked Critical, Significant, Minor. Claude shows you the findings before it fixes anything.

Fixing what it finds

Claude runs /conexus:rdr-fix, which shows each finding with the place in the document it refers to, and then fixes them. Two rules apply, and you will see Claude follow them:

  • A fix changes a fact, not a sentence. If a count was wrong, every place that count appears changes, including memory.
  • A fix adds no new explanation. New prose is where the next round's findings come from. A fact that needs support goes into a research finding first, and the RDR sentence is copied from it.

Then Claude runs the gate again. That is round two.

The budget

Two rounds, three if a round changed the design. If findings are still arriving at the end of the budget, the RDR is done and the remaining findings are residuals: recorded, fixed once after the pass, not re-gated. From round three on, fix only the finding that actually blocks shipping.

What happens without the budget, measured. RDR-204, September 2026: ten gate rounds in 24 hours, no design change after round four. Every finding after that was in text the previous fix had written. RDR-205, the same week: five rounds and twelve fix checks, and the file grew from 844 to 1,870 lines with no design change after round two. Four independent runs of the same check on the same commit agreed on one finding out of eleven. A gate that runs five rounds is examining itself. The budget, and a fix check that counts a defect only when two of three independent readers raise it, were added after those two weeks. They are the reason the loop no longer runs, not a reason it never did.

The signal to stop is yours. Pick a word for it and use it. Claude will keep going otherwise, because each round produces findings and findings look like work.

When it passes

Claude reports PASSED and the residuals, if any. If the pass came with Significant findings fixed in place, Claude pauses and shows you the fixes before anything else. Read them. Then lesson 6.

6

Accept, then break it into work

15 min

Accepting is a decision, and it is yours. After it, the RDR stops being a draft and becomes the reference that the code is built against. Then Claude turns the Implementation Plan into tracked tasks, in phases, so the build can happen across many sessions without losing the thread.

Accept

You might say
Accept RDR <NUMBER>.

Claude runs /conexus:rdr-accept. The status becomes accepted. Memory records the acceptance. From now on the RDR is not edited to match what gets built. If the build proves it wrong, lesson 7 says what happens.

Decompose

You might say
Break RDR <NUMBER> into tasks by phase. Audit the plan once before I look at it.
What Claude does

Claude uses the planning skill (/conexus:create-plan) to turn each phase of the Implementation Plan into tasks in the bd task tracker, with dependencies between them, under one epic that names the RDR. Then it runs the plan audit (/conexus:plan-audit), which checks the tasks against the code as it is: do the files exist, is the order possible, is anything assumed that the code contradicts. It shows you the task tree.

The plan audit has the same budget rule as the gate. One round, two at most. Past that, the first task's first test run tells you more than a third audit would.

Read the tree, not the tasks

Look at the phases and the dependencies. Is the first phase the one that proves the design works, or the one that is easiest? It should be the first. Is there a phase that ships nothing visible? Ask why. Then say go, or move things.

Investigate further

  • Ask Claude: "Show the task tree for RDR <NUMBER>, and which task is ready to start."
  • The planning skill: how phases become tasks.
7

Build it, across many sessions

20 min

The build takes days or weeks, in many sessions. Two things hold it together: the continuation at the end of every session, and the phase review at the end of every phase. The RDR is the fixed reference the whole time. This lesson is about the rhythm, not about writing code.

A session in the build

  1. Start the session by pasting the continuation from the last one. Claude reads it, checks the branch, and picks the ready task.
  2. Claude implements the task. The plugin's guidance has it write the test first, then the code, then ask for review.
  3. For the review, Claude starts the reviewer agents: one for the code, one critic for the design fit. It fixes what they find and shows you the rest.
  4. Claude closes the task. The task tracker knows what is done. Memory records any decision made along the way.
  5. Before you stop, /conexus:continuation. Always.

You are present for the decisions and absent for the typing. A useful habit: at the start of each session, ask "where are we on RDR <NUMBER>?" before the paste. Compare the answer with the continuation. They should agree.

The end of a phase

You might say
Phase <N> looks done. Run the phase review.
What Claude does

Claude runs /conexus:phase-review-gate. It reads the RDR's Approach from top to bottom, lists every concrete thing the Approach says this phase delivers, and finds the closed task that shipped each one. Anything named in the Approach with no task is one of three things: built somewhere else (Claude shows where), deferred to a named task, or missing. Missing means the phase is not done. This is the check that stops scope from shrinking quietly, one small omission at a time.

Code review and test validation cannot catch this. They check what was built. Only a reading of the RDR against the tasks checks what was not built.

When the build proves the RDR wrong

Do not edit the accepted RDR. If a phase shows that a decision does not work, say so, and have Claude mark the RDR abandoned or superseded and start a new one with what was learned. The new RDR is short, because most of the research still holds. The old one stays as the record of what was believed and why. An accepted RDR that is edited to match the code is not a record of anything.

Small things are different. A file name that changed, a detail the Approach did not specify: those are noted in the task and in memory, not in the RDR. The test is whether the decision changed.

Status and code are two facts. An RDR's status says what was decided. It says nothing about whether code exists. A day spent on tooling measured against an RDR, or on documents about it, reads in a status line like a day spent building it. On 2026-09-10 the Nexus project ended a day believing RDR-205 was accepted and built. It was a draft with no code. Ask, at any point: "Does RDR <NUMBER> have code yet, and on which branch?" The answer is one sentence, and Claude should give it in every status report without being asked.

  • The phase review gate: the cross-walk and the incident that produced it.
  • Ask Claude, mid-build: "What does the Approach of RDR <NUMBER> promise that has no task yet?" Do this before the phase review, not after.
8

Close it, and what remains

10 min

Closing is the last decision, and yours. It does three things: it settles every task that is still open, it records what the build taught, and it moves the RDR into the knowledge store so that the next decision finds it.

Before you close

You might say
RDR <NUMBER> is built. What is still open under it?

Claude lists every task under the RDR's epic that is not closed: open, deferred, blocked. For each one you decide: close it because it is done, defer it to a named later piece of work, or keep the RDR open until it is done. Claude does not close a blocker to make the gate pass. If it proposes to, refuse.

Close

You might say
Close RDR <NUMBER>, implemented. Write the post-mortem.
What Claude does

Claude runs /conexus:rdr-close. The status becomes closed. If you asked for it, Claude writes a short post-mortem: what the build changed compared with the plan, what took longer, what was learned. Then the RDR is indexed into the knowledge store. Memory records the close.

A post-mortem is worth writing when the build taught something the RDR did not predict. When it did not, the close is a status flip and nothing more, and that is correct.

What remains

The RDR is now part of the corpus. The next time you or Claude research a similar question, in this project or another that shares the store, the search finds it, and the research agent reads it before it reads the web. This is the point of the whole cycle. One RDR is a decision. Two hundred are a memory of how the project thinks.

The cycle again, in one line. A conversation becomes a draft. Research grows it in cycles. You read and react, sometimes with a critic. A gate checks it, within a budget. You accept. Claude breaks it into phases. Sessions build it, each ending with a continuation, each phase ending with a review. You close it. It becomes something the next RDR can find.

Investigate further

  • After a close, ask Claude to research something adjacent to the RDR's subject. Watch it find the RDR.
  • The close skill: the task gate and the post-mortem template.
  • The Nexus RDR index: every RDR of the project with its status. Pick a closed one and read its post-mortem.

Complete

You know what an RDR is for and what it is not. You know that it begins in conversation, grows through research cycles, and is read and changed as a rendering. You know that the gate is for the decisions that are expensive to reverse and that it has a budget when it is used, that accepting and closing are your decisions, and that the build runs across sessions held together by continuations and phase reviews. You know that an RDR's status says where the decision is, not whether the code exists, and to ask for both. And you know that an accepted RDR is never edited to fit the code: it is abandoned and succeeded.
